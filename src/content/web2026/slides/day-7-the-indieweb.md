## If code is incidental, why are we spending three weeks on it?

Because web development inherently makes you aware of the constraints and possibilities of your chosen medium. 


Even in a drag-and-drop editor, you have to think structurally. You have to ask questions.

note: What screen am I targeting? Will it work in another browser? Will it work in one year? It is not about having answers within 3 weeks, but raising these questions bonds you to the medium.

## What kind of room is a website?

This is Laurel Schwulst's question(cardboard box lady from Day 2) , and I think it is the best question anyone has asked about personal websites.

<!-- Add screenshot of the essay header: https://thecreativeindependent.com/essays/laurel-schwulst-my-website-is-a-shifting-house-next-to-a-river-of-knowledge-what-could-yours-be/ -->

---

In her essay, her website is a **shifting house next to a river of knowledge**.

The website is a _place_. 

---

A website can also be, she suggests:

- a **room** you rearrange for different occasions
- a **shelf** holding a few careful things
- a **plant** that grows slowly if you water it
- a **puddle** that reflects whoever looks into it


None of these are "personal brand" <!--element class="fragment"-->

---

### On the platforms, no one is looking out for you but you

Schwulst points out that the anxiety you feel on social media is not a personal failing. These are companies that prioritise advertisers over you. Feeling weird there is the correct response.

<br/>

A personal website is where you get to _set the record straight_ on who you are.

## Principles of the IndieWeb

Well, some broad philosophies anyway.

## Own Your Data

Plant your content into the field of your own website. When you migrate to a new host (Netlify) or CMS (something like Wordpress), if your site stays on the same domain, everyone will still find you.

---

### Cook what you want. Eat what you cook.

If you design for some hypothetical user, they may not actually exist; if you make for yourself, you actually do exist.

- A website for your two-line thoughts every week?
- A website for each of your course modules?
- A set of pages to document your learning a new tool?

### Always growing

Gardens are never finished, they’re **constantly growing, evolving, and changing**. Just like a real soil, carrot, and cabbage garden.

---

![](assets/slides/day-7-the-indieweb/IMG-20250729001846131.png)

note: You’re freed from the pressure to get everything right immediately. You can test ideas, get feedback, and revise your opinions like a good internet citizen. It’s _low friction_. Gardening your thoughts becomes a daily ritual that only takes a small amount of effort. Over time, big things grow. It gives readers an insight into your writing and thinking process. They come to realise you are not a magical idea machine banging out perfectly formed thoughts, but instead an equally mediocre human doing The Work of trying to understand the world and make sense of it alongside you.

---

### Imperfection & Learning in Public

Putting anything imperfect and half-written on an “official website” may feel strange.

<br/>

We have all been trained to behave like tiny, performative corporations when it comes to presenting ourselves in digital space.

note: Digital gardening is the [Domestic Cozy](https://www.ribbonfarm.com/series/domestic-cozy/) response to the professional personal blog; it’s both intimate and public, weird and welcoming. It’s less performative than a blog, but more intentional and thoughtful than a Twitter feed. It wants to build personal knowledge over time, rather than engage in banter and quippy conversations.

# In order to connect dots, one must first have the dots

## Planting the seeds for our gardens



---

## Phase 1: Personal Archaeology

You already have more dots than you think.

---

**Part 1: The dump**

List everything that falls into these categories:

- Things you've learned that surprised you
- Tools/methods you use that others might not know about
- Processes you've figured out through trial and error
- Opinions you hold that seem unpopular
- Projects you're working on (even tiny ones)
- Collections you're building (even mental ones)

Don't edit yourself. Just dump.

---

**Part 2: The dig**

- Put each brain dump item on a separate sticky note (whether IRL or FigJam is up to you)
- Arrange them in some space, what clusters together?
- What connects to what? Draw lines.
- What sits in the center with the most connections?
- What sits alone but still interests you?

## Document your roaming

### Field Notes for Visiting a Garden

1. What is the very first thing this person assumes you already know?
2. Where is the newest thing on the site? How did you find it?
3. Find something unfinished or half-grown. How is it marked?
4. When was this site last tended? What are the clues?
5. What is one navigation choice here that no template would ever make?
6. Where does the author's voice leak through the most?
7. **What would break if this site had to live on Instagram instead?**
8. Steal one idea.

notes: When you can say _exactly_ what a platform would flatten out of a site you love, you understand the politics of the indie web better than any manifesto can teach you.

## Phase 3: Return to your wall

Come back to your sticky notes with your field notes in hand.

- Which of your clusters could become a _plot_ in your garden?
- Does the idea you stole (question 8) fit anywhere on your wall?
- What kind of room, shelf, plant, or puddle is forming here?

---

## Phase 4: Mapping

---

#### How do people wander through your space?

**Part 1**

Someone lands on your site for the first time. In 10 seconds, they need to understand:

- Who you are
- What you care about
- Why they should stick around

---

**Part 2**

- Using your clusters, sketch your site structure
- How do things connect to each other?
- What are your main sections?
- How does someone discover your best work?
- How do you handle works-in-progress vs. finished thoughts?

Draw this as a map, not a hierarchy.

---
## The 15-minute Tuesday test

For every plot you're considering, ask:


**"Could I add to this in 15 minutes, on a random Tuesday, when I am tired?"** <!--element class="fragment"-->

If no, it is a Project, not a plot. <!--element class="fragment"-->

---

## Writing your manifesto

1. Commit to your **three plots**
2. Draft your 200-300 word **manifesto**:
    - _Why am I making this space?_
    - _What kind of room is it? What does it have, why does it have it? What is your philosophy?_
    - _What tone fits me?_
    - _How will this space grow?_

Some manifestos to look at: https://yesterweb.org/#appendix-i
